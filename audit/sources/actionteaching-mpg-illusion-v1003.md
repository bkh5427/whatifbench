The MPG Illusion: How Cognitive Biases Fuel Climate Change
The in-class quiz, based on Study 1 of Larrick and Soll (2008), can be distributed as a handout
They are asked to rank five vehicle upgrades in order of their benefit to the environment, using "1" for the largest gas savings and "5" for the smallest.
(A) Upgrading mileage from 18 to 28 MPG
(B) Upgrading mileage from 16 to 20 MPG
(C) Upgrading mileage from 34 to 50 MPG
(D) Upgrading mileage from 20 to 22 MPG
(E) Upgrading mileage from 42 to 46 MPG
People incorrectly believe that gas savings and greenhouse gas reductions are a linear function of increases in MPG.
However, the actual relationship is curvilinear.
The MPG illusion is corrected when fuel efficiency is expressed in terms of GPM such as 'gallons per 10,000 miles.'
In the case of the MPG illusion, a clear policy implication is to have the government and consumer magazines supplement MPG information with GPM measures (e.g., carbon emissions per 100 miles).
Larrick, R. P., & Soll, J. B. (2008). The MPG illusion. Science, 320, 1593-1594.
